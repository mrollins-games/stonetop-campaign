150  All the tales agree: the Folk were in the  Great Wood   (page 200) long before Stonetop was founded. They welcomed the newcomers, or at least suffered them, but demanded that villagers never fell a living tree from the Wood. The Forest Folk mostly kept to themselves. But over generations, contacts were made. Favors exchanged, friendships won, stories shared. And more shared, too, judging by the odd eyes that some villagers bear. The villagers did not notice when the Forest Folk left, not right away. Encounters were never common. Then they became rare. Then there were none at all. Their absence echoes through the Great Wood. Their crude glyphs can still be found, carved into trees and boulders. Their sacred places stand empty. And dangerous creatures—things once hunted by the Folk—now breed freely. Forest eyes  Those who inherit the “forest eyes” see well at night. They see by mere starlight as well as most humans can see under a full moon; they see by moonlight as well as most humans can see at dusk. Beyond that, certain plants, lichens, and rocks shine with indescribable colors that can only be seen with the forest eyes. The Folk would sometimes be seen staring rapt at a mundane grove or meadow, marveling at some unseen beauty. Humans with the forest eyes sometimes do likewise. If a PC was born with (or develops) the forest eyes, feel free to use these GM moves on them:  ä   Reveal glyphs, markings, or patterns that only they can see  ä   Distract them with colors and patterns of impossible beauty  Glyphs and pictograms  The Forest Folk left crude marks and drawings throughout the Great Wood. When the PCs encounter a point of inter- est in the Great Wood, and it was around before the Folk disappeared, pick or have someone roll the Die of Fate:  1d6   markings  1-2 None to be found 3   Hidden, incomplete/marred 4   Hidden, intact 5   Obvious but incomplete/marred 6   Obvious and intact “Hidden” glyphs might be hidden inten- tionally, obscured by happenstance (vines, growth, etc.), and/or made with pigments that only the forest eyes can see. To an outsider, these glyphs and picto- grams seem like nonsense—arbitrary shapes and crude, stylized drawings. But the Forest Folk had an elaborate visual vocabulary, and most of these markings are rich with meaning. A PC can Know Things to attempt to discern the meanings of any such glyphs, with disadvantage if they are incomplete or marred.   On a 7-9 , they discern a vague concept, like “dangerous plants,” “bound spirit” or “sorrow/loss”.
