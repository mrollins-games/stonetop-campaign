98  Fae nature  The Fae are living beings made of spirit- stuff. They aren’t quite spirits, but interact with spirits as if they were. They’re made of flesh and bone, or close enough, but it is strange, malleable, and resilient. They… …   need not eat or drink, but can enjoy such fare; …   need not sleep, but often choose to; …   can suffocate or drown, but usually recover when they can breathe again; …   feel no drive to reproduce, but might enjoy sex, fall in love, sire or bear chil- dren, steal them from mortals; …   do not grow feeble with age, but can grow bored, deeply set in their ways; …   cannot break a promise or an oath, but are keen at finding loopholes; and …   feel the weight of debts and insults as though they were heavy chains.  Injury  Fae are unnaturally tough (Armor 4) and can heal any wound not caused by iron. A Fae who suffers a “mortal” wound might pass through the Last Door. But they might also change their mind and come back later, for Fae are fickle things.  Iron  Iron (including steel) is offensive to Fae. Its presence insults them. It disrupts their magic. It burns their skin, ignores their Armor, leaves scars that never fade. Iron can kill a Fae, truly kill them dead.  Black iron   (page 307) is even worse. Its presence unravels their magic. Its touch doesn’t just burn, it suppurates their flesh (+1d6 damage, ignores armor, messy). It leaves vicious, gaping wounds that never heal and that eventually turn them into  Hollow Fae   (page 114).  Magic and the Fae  Fae are Fae, neither spirit nor mortal. As a rule, magic that specifically affects “spirits” or “mortals” does not apply to the Fae. They are their own thing. With that said, magic is fuzzy and each Fae is unique. When in doubt, consider what feels right in this particular case. A ploy that would work in a fairy tale should probably work against the Fae!  Illusions and glamours  Illusions are lies. They have no substance. An illusionary chair bears no weight; the illusion of a sword does not cut. A skillful Fae might create the illusion of pain and blood when you are cut with an illusionary sword, but the wound has no lasting effect. Glamour is more profound. It changes a thing and how it interacts with the world, at least until the glamour fades. Glamour a rock into a chair and sit comfortably. Glamour a stick into a sword and it will cut, make you bleed real blood. When the glamour ends, the wound remains. Glamours are like poetry. One might glamour an acorn into a gem, or fluttering leaves into butterflies, or a braying mortal’s head into that of a donkey. But one does not just, say, glamour a spear into a puddle of water. These things must rhyme! Fae can see through each other’s illusions and glamours, but as a rule they play along. Doing otherwise can be insulting. Glamour affects neither iron nor steel, and the touch of either causes illusions to flick- er and fade. The touch of   black iron   (page 307) dispels both illusions and glamours, like soap dropped in oily water.  Fae Fae
