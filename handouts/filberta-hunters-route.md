## D8 — The Rooted Pass

You were twelve. Your father woke you before dawn — "Up. We're going deep today." You were than thrilled not to be abandoned. We'll see for how long. 

The pass is a steep hillside, almost a cliff, choked with ancient roots — gnarled, interlocking, black when wet. The gaps between them drop into unseen tangles. There is no obvious path. But there IS a path, if you grew up climbing it.

Third root from the split oak — that one holds. The mossy one below it is rotten; skip it. Place your foot where the two thick roots cross — there's a ledge. The flat grey root that looks stable? Hollow. Step on the edge, not the centre.

You were fast that morning. Faster than your father. You were already at the bottom, hopping foot to foot in the dark old-growth while he picked his way down the last section. He grunted. "Eager today."

You were. The sloes were ripe.

**What you know:**
- You could do this blindfolded. You've climbed the Rooted Pass since you were seven. When the expidtion gets there you don't roll defy danger to descend - hah.
- Most people who attempt it turn back. It's not marked. It doesn't want to be found. Looks impossible to most.
- At the base, the forest opens into massive old-growth trunks spaced wider. Game trails cut everywhere. This is where the hunting begins — for those who hunt.

## E7 — The Coppice

### Morning

The sloes were ripe. You'd been thinking about them for a week — the bushes at the south edge of the coppice, heavy with dark blue fruit, the first ones always the sweetest because the frost hadn't found them yet. You left your father at the pass and ran.

The forest changed around you as you went deeper. Undergrowth thinning. Massive trunks in deliberate patterns — oaks, beeches, hazel growing in layers. The ground carpeted in acorn caps and beech husks. You didn't think about WHY it was like this. You just knew it was yours. Your cathedral. Your larder. The place that always provided.

You found the sloe bushes and ate until your teeth were purple.

And then you heard them.

### The Canopy Parliament

Chattering. Not the sharp alarm-bark you knew from Stonetop's common reds — this was *conversation*. Dozens of voices, overlapping, rising and falling like people arguing in a tavern. You looked up.

The canopy was alive with them. Red-furred bodies pouring through the branches — leaping, chasing, clustering on broad oak limbs in groups of four and five. Tails flicking in patterns. Heads tilting together. One would chitter rapidly while others listened, ears forward, intent. Then another would respond. Then three at once.

They looked like a council in session. Absurdly serious. Tiny furry lawmakers debating policy on a mossy branch.

You laughed out loud. "Parliament," you said to no one. "It's a parliament."

They froze when they heard you laugh. Every head turned. Dozens of dark eyes staring down.

You held very still. Held out a sloe on your palm.

One — smaller than the rest, bold — dropped to a low branch. Stared at you. You didn't move. It descended further. Took the sloe. Ate it, watching you the whole time. Came back for another.

By midmorning you were sitting cross-legged beneath the oak with eight of them on the ground around you, sharing sloes and hazelnuts from your pocket, chattering at them softly while they chatted back. You named the bold one. You named three others. You were perfectly, incandescently happy.

### Why no one else comes here

You've thought about this — later, older, looking back. The hunting here is the best in the Great Wood. Better than anywhere within a day's walk of Stonetop. But the route stays quiet. Just the hunters use it regularly. Why?

- **Hunters don't share hunter things with non-hunters.** It's part of the code. You don't point newcomers at your grounds. "Game's good in the deep wood" — that's all anyone says. The WHERE stays with those who earned it.
- **The Parliament keeps it that way.** Anyone who doesn't already know the route gets spotted by the relay. The alarm cascades. Game disperses before they're within bowshot. They come once, catch nothing, and don't come back. The forest self-selects for the patient. For the quiet. For *you.*
- **There's nothing to describe.** You've tried to tell Halpin about this place more than once. But the words go sideways every time. You end up talking about the squirrels. About the way the light moves. About how it FEELS. The place itself — what it IS, what it gives — doesn't survive translation.
- **Your father guards beyond it.**  He's possessive of his camp in a way he's possessive of nothing else.

### Evening

You lost track of time. The Parliament eventually scattered — not alarmed, just done with you for the day. Off to do squirrel things. You lay in the leaf litter watching the canopy sway and the light go gold, then amber, then dim.

Dark was coming. Your father wasn't back.

He was supposed to collect you hours ago. 

You went to find him. South, toward E5. Toward his camp.

## E5 — The Camp

You smelled smoke before you saw the bower — Dilwen's hazel camp at the narrow northern tip of the glade. The oiled hide over bent poles. The fire pit. You'd been here a hundred times.

He was crouching by the fire. And beside him — a cage. Woven hazel rods, freshly made. Inside it, pressed together in a terrified huddle:

A family. A mother and three kits. Red fur. Dark eyes. Tails wrapped around each other.

Squirrels. HIS traps had caught squirrels.

Your father looked up at you. No guilt. No hesitation. "Good pelt price on reds this season." He said it like he was telling you the weather.

You couldn't speak. You stood there with sloe-stained fingers and the memory of the Parliament's trust still warm in your chest, and you couldn't speak. The kits were crying — high, thin sounds you'd never heard a squirrel make before. The mother was throwing herself against the hazel rods.

You wanted to open the cage. You wanted to scream at him. You wanted to break his hands.

You couldn't do any of it. He was your father. He was bigger. He was *him*. And you were twelve.

### Your Squirrel

One of the kits — the smallest — had pressed itself into the corner of the cage furthest from Dilwen. It wasn't crying. It was watching you. Directly. Intently. The same bold stare as the one who'd taken the sloe from your palm that morning.

You don't know if it was the same squirrel. You've never been sure. But it looked at you like it knew you. Like it had decided something.

That night, while your father slept and you lay awake sick with anger, the smallest kit squeezed through a gap in the hazel rods — too tight for its mother, too tight for its siblings, but just barely possible for the runt. It crossed the ground between the cage and your bedroll. It climbed onto your chest. It sat there, looking at you, and it didn't leave.

In the morning your father saw it on your shoulder. He shrugged. "Runt wouldn't've survived anyway."

You've never forgiven him. For any of it.

The others — the mother and the remaining kits — he sold the pelts in town three days later. You know this. You try not to think about it.

**What you know:**
- Your squirrel chose you. Not the other way around.
- When you bring it back to E7, the Parliament recognises it immediately. They cluster around it. Calm. Welcoming. It sits on a familiar branch and chatters softly with the others. It's catching up. It's home.
- It doesn't go manic here. It settles.
- Your father has never once successfully trapped a squirrel in E7 since that day. Not one. The Parliament remembers.

### Running

You couldn't stay. Not another minute. Not with him breathing ten feet away and the cage still sitting by the dead fire with three shapes huddled inside. You got up before dawn. You walked south through the glade — past the wide mouth of the lightning bolt, past the wildflowers and the soft grass, down to the river's edge.

You'd never crossed here. The river runs fast and cold. Chest-high in summer. The current is vicious — undertow that drags at your legs, algae-slick rocks, sudden holes. Most townsfolk won't attempt it. You'd always gone back north, up through D8, the long way home.

Not today. Today you needed to be away from him. Today the long way was impossible.

## D4 — The Crossing

You waded in. The cold hit you like a wall. The squirrel on your shoulder dug its claws in and pressed flat against your neck. The current shoved — harder than you expected — and your feet slipped on the smooth stones. Water up to your chest. Then your shoulders. The far bank looked impossibly far.

You were angry enough not to care.

Halfway across, the undertow grabbed your legs and pulled. Your feet left the bottom. For one terrible second you were just a girl in a river with no ground beneath her, and the squirrel was screaming in your ear, and the current was dragging you downstream —

And then something pushed you. Firm. Steady. A current beneath the current, moving *across* the river instead of down it. Pushing you toward the far bank. Your feet found rock — solid, stable, exactly where you needed it. Another step. Another. The water dropped from your shoulders to your chest to your waist.

You made it. You stood on the D4 bank dripping and shaking, the squirrel puffed up like a wet pinecone on your shoulder, chattering furiously at the river. Something had guided you — guided you home, back to kin, to the greatest safety it knew to give.

You didn't understand it then. You still don't, really. But you made it home that day — soaked, freezing, alone except for a stolen squirrel kit who'd chosen you over its cage. Your father didn't come home until the next evening. He never asked how you got back. He never noticed you'd crossed the river.

**What you know:**
- The crossing is dangerous but not impossible. You've done it that once.
- Something in the water helped you. You don't know what. But it pushed you toward home — like it understood that's where you needed to be.
- You've never told anyone about that crossing. It felt private. Desperate. Not something you want to explain.
- Townsfolk don't cross here. When they try, they don't make it — current pushes them back. You don't know why it let YOU through.

## The Offering (what hunters know)

When you make a kill, you leave an offering. Every hunter knows this. It's the first thing you're taught — or the first thing you learn from watching. You leave something: a portion of the meat, arranged a certain way, left at the kill site. You don't ask what it's for. You just do it.

The legend among hunters: if someone who doesn't know the offering were to hunt, they'd be thinned from the herd. This is why non-hunters don't hunt. It's not a rule anyone enforces — it enforces itself.

Your father traps instead of hunts. The animals die when he isn't there. He thinks this means he doesn't have to make the offering. He thinks he's clever.

You think he's a coward.

## The Malachite Formations (E7, NE edge)

### How you found them

You were eleven — the year before the squirrel day. Your father left you in E7 as usual. You waited for squirrels. They didn't come. (Your father was too close — you know that now.) You got bored.

You walked further NE than you'd ever gone. The managed trees thinned. The undergrowth changed — wilder, older. And then you saw them.

Malachite formations. Waist-high, translucent, banded in swirling layers of dark and light green — shaped like crashing waves caught mid-break, spray frozen as solid arcs. They ran in a line as far as you could see, curving away into the trees in both directions. Deep green, rich as velvet, catching the filtered canopy light and holding it.

You touched one. Cool. Impossibly smooth. Faintly humming.

You knew — in the way children know things without being told — that this was a border. The other side was different. You crossed it anyway.
